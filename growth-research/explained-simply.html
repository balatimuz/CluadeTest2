Picture 2

Why ₹9, and not free?

If the ₹9 makes no money, why not make the workshop free? Because the ₹9 does four jobs that “free” cannot do.

1

It proves the person can pay

Someone who completes a UPI payment has money, a working phone, and the habit of paying online. A free sign-up proves nothing. The ₹9 filters out people who will never pay ₹40,000.

2

It gets a verified phone number

UPI payment = real mobile number. Now they can message that person on WhatsApp for 3 days with reminders and stories. WhatsApp messages get read by 8 in 10 people; emails by 2 in 10.

3

It teaches Facebook who buys

When you run ads, you tell Facebook “show this to people likely to buy”. Facebook can only learn that if you tell it who bought. Every ₹9 payment is one lesson. Thousands of cheap lessons a week means Facebook gets very good at finding buyers. This is the single biggest reason.

4

People show up for what they paid for

Paid workshops: about half attend. Free ones: about a quarter. And once someone has said “yes” with money once, saying yes again feels natural. Small yes → big yes.

The number is made up — it's a recording that thousands watch at once. Fear of missing out makes you act now instead of thinking.

Refresh the page and it resets. Its only job is to stop you from saying “let me think about it”.

If so many people did it, it must be fine. The Amazon/Google logos are usually where attendees work, not partners.

“IIT founders · in the news”authority

Most of the “news” is paid press releases republished by newspapers. Looks like journalism, works like an ad.

“Laid off? AI took your job?”fear

The ad you saw was literally labelled “Laid off Employees” and aimed at “Healthcare Operations, age 30–65” in its own link. Scared people click more than curious people.

“Made for healthcare professionals”personalisation

Same workshop, different first sentence for nurses, bankers, teachers, HR. Feels made for you → cheaper for them.

“Offer ends when this call ends”deadline + EMI

After 90 minutes of genuinely useful teaching you feel you owe them. Then: ₹40,000, only right now, in easy EMIs. A phone sales team calls everyone who didn't buy for the next week.

The honest part

Why you should not copy this

It is not a software business. It is a selling business. What makes Be10x work is not the workshop — it is:

  • a team making dozens of new video ads every week,
  • a phone sales team paid on commission,
  • a brand willing to live with 1-star reviews and government notices (India's ad regulator fines “fake urgency” and “guaranteed job” claims; education is the most-complained-about ad category every year),
  • and 3 years of data teaching Facebook who buys, plus a ₹10-crore-a-year ad budget you would be bidding against.

Also: it is getting harder. Getting one ₹9 sign-up cost ₹100 in 2020 and costs ₹250–₹1,000 now, because everyone copied it and people have learned what “₹9 workshop” means.

The plan · in order

Six steps. Each one only makes sense after the one before.

1

Don't touch ads yet. Give it to 5 businesses for free.

Pick one type of business — say dental clinics — and one of your bots. Find 5 clinics through people you know. Install it free. Every week ask: “what did this save you?” Write down the number, e.g. “recovered 11 missed appointments”. That number becomes your ad later. Nothing in marketing works without it.

Cost: ₹0 · Time: 2–3 weeks
2

Put a small price on it. Not free.

₹499 to set it up on the clinic's own number for 14 days, then ₹2,499 a month if they keep it. The ₹499 is your version of the ₹9: it filters out time-wasters, gets you a real phone number, and teaches Facebook who buys. Honest version, same effect.

3

Make one 15-second vertical video.

Screen-record your bot handling one real message. Text on top: “Dr. Priya's clinic missed 23 appointments last month. Now WhatsApp handles it. Tap to try.” The button opens a WhatsApp chat with your bot. The person plays with it. The bot is the demo, the demo is the sales pitch. No landing page needed.

Cost: ₹0 · Time: 1 day
4

Spend ₹1,000 a day for two weeks.

In Facebook's ad tool, choose: goal = Sales (never “Traffic” or “Clicks” — Facebook will find you 10,000 people who click and never buy), where = WhatsApp, show it on WhatsApp Status and Instagram Reels, let Facebook pick the audience with a hint like “dentist” or “clinic owner”, only your city and two nearby. When someone pays ₹499, tell Facebook (your payment tool can do this automatically) — that is the “teaching Facebook who buys” part.

Cost: ₹14,000 total
5

Look at only three numbers.

① What did each WhatsApp chat cost you? Want: under ₹80. ② Of the people who chatted, how many paid ₹499? Want: more than 5 in 100. ③ Of the ₹499 payers, how many stayed for ₹2,499? Want: more than 4 in 10. Whichever number fails tells you exactly what to fix: bad ① = better video; bad ② = better bot demo; bad ③ = better first-two-weeks experience.

6

If all three numbers are good, spend more. If not, fix one thing.

Raise the budget slowly (about 20% every few days). Add a second city. Then a second type of business — coaching centres, timed for the Feb–July admission rush. Do not build new features nobody asked for. Do not go “for any business” — a dentist buys “WhatsApp receptionist for dental clinics” in one conversation; nobody buys “AI WhatsApp automation platform”.

Target by day 90: 5+ paying clinics ≈ ₹12,500 / month, growing

Vocabulary, in brackets

Ad (a video or picture you pay Facebook to show people)
Meta / Facebook Ads Manager (the website where you create ads for Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp — all one company)
WhatsApp Status ad (an ad that appears between your friends' Status updates)
Click-to-WhatsApp ad (an ad whose button opens a WhatsApp chat with the business)
Impression (one person saw the ad once)
CPM (what 1,000 impressions cost you — ₹50 to ₹400 in India)
Click / tap (someone touched the ad)
CPC (what one tap costs you — ₹5 to ₹25 on WhatsApp Status)
Landing page (the one page people arrive at after tapping — one message, one button)
Conversion (the person did the thing you wanted: paid, registered, started a chat)
Lead (a person who gave you their phone number)
CPL (what one phone number cost you in ads)
Customer (a person who actually paid for the real product)
CAC (what one paying customer cost you in ads)
LTV (total money one customer gives you before they leave)
ROAS (money back ÷ money spent on ads; 2× means ₹100 of ads brought ₹200)
Churn (how many paying customers cancel each month)
Funnel (the whole path from “saw ad” to “paid”, narrowing at each step)
Low-ticket / tripwire (a tiny first purchase like ₹9 or ₹499 whose job is to get a first “yes”)
High-ticket (the expensive thing that actually makes the money — ₹35,000+)
Upsell (selling something more expensive to someone who already bought)
OTO / order bump (an extra offer shown right after payment, “only on this page”)
Nurture (the messages between “signed up” and “bought” — reminders, stories, proof)
Show-up rate (of the people who registered, how many actually came)
Close rate (of the people who came, how many bought)
Creative (the actual ad video or image)
Hook (the first 2 seconds that stop the thumb)
Targeting (telling Facebook who should see the ad — age, city, interests, job)
Advantage+ audience (letting Facebook's computer pick who sees it instead of you)
Campaign objective (what you ask Facebook to find you: clicks, phone numbers, or buyers — always pick buyers)
Pixel / Conversions API (the way your website or payment tool tells Facebook “this person just paid”)
Anchoring (showing a big price first so the real price looks small)
Scarcity / urgency (“only 137 seats”, countdown timers — pressure to act now)
Social proof (“5 lakh learners”, star ratings, logos — “others did it, so it's safe”)
Vertical (one specific type of business, like dental clinics)
SaaS (software people pay for every month, like Netflix for businesses)
MRR (all your monthly subscriptions added up)
Productized service (a fixed job at a fixed price: “WhatsApp bot set-up, ₹25,000”)
BSP (a company that resells WhatsApp's official business messaging — AiSensy, Wati, Gupshup)
ASCI / CCPA (India's advertising watchdog and consumer-protection authority; they fine fake urgency and false promises)
